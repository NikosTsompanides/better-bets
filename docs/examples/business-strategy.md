# Business strategy: moving upmarket

A hard-to-reverse bet where backcasting did the useful work.

## bet-frame

**Asked:** "Should we move upmarket?"

**Reframed:** *Where does the next £2m of ARR come from at a cost of sale we
can actually fund?* Upmarket is one answer. Expansion revenue and a cheaper
acquisition channel are others.

**Facts.** ARR £3.1m. CAC payback 14 months in SMB. Two enterprise inbounds
last quarter, both stalled on SSO and audit logging.

**Assumptions.** Enterprise deals close in six months — no data, borrowed from
a competitor's blog post. The product is "basically ready" bar SSO. The team
can sell enterprise. Existing SMB revenue holds while attention moves.

**Opinion.** That SMB is "a treadmill." Strongly felt, and load-bearing.

## bet-evaluate

**Confidence.** Low. Almost every input is an assumption, and the two that
matter most — cycle length and whether this team can sell enterprise — have no
evidence at all.

**Outside view.** Companies moving upmarket typically underestimate sales cycle
by roughly double and discover the product gap is compliance and procurement,
not features.

**Stakes.** Reorients hiring, roadmap, and pricing. Blast radius is the whole
company. Cost of delay is real but not acute.

**Reversibility.** Hard. Enterprise commitments create support obligations and
a roadmap that no longer fits SMB.

**Backcasting.** *It is 18 months on and this failed. What happened?* We built
SSO and audit logging in five months, not two. The first three deals took nine
months each. Meanwhile SMB churn rose because the roadmap stopped serving them,
and we ended the year with less revenue and two segments served badly.

That story is specific enough to design against — and the detectable early
signal is the *first* deal's cycle length, not the revenue number.

**Size.** Low confidence, high downside, hard to reverse → investigate.

## bet-experiment

**Deciding uncertainty:** can this team close an enterprise deal, and how long
does it take?

**Probe:** pursue the two stalled inbounds properly with a manual, unscalable
compliance workaround rather than building anything. Cap at one quarter.

**Criteria.** One deal closed or credibly near-close within the quarter →
proceed and build properly. Both stalled on things a workaround cannot reach →
the gap is structural, not feature-shaped.

## bet-update

**Committed:** run the probe, freeze the reorganisation until it reports.
**Update criteria.** Either deal reaching contract → build SSO. Both stalling
on procurement rather than product → the constraint is not the product, and the
strategy needs rethinking rather than resourcing. Review at quarter end.

## bet-review

One closed at month five, one stalled on procurement. The team proceeded, and
revenue landed roughly on plan.

**Good decision, good outcome** — but the review named the near-miss: the
backcast predicted the nine-month cycle almost exactly, and the only reason it
did not bite was that one deal happened to be unusually motivated. The lesson
is not "moving upmarket works."

**Belief updated:** this team's enterprise cycle is five to nine months, not
six as assumed. Plan the next one on the range, not the point estimate.
