# A personal decision: the job offer

The framework is not about work. This is the domain where resulting is hardest
to resist, because the outcome is your life.

## bet-frame

**Asked:** "Should I take the offer?"

**Reframed:** *What do I want the next two years to look like, and which
available option gets closest?* The offer is one option. Renegotiating where I
am, and staying unchanged, are others — and staying is the one people forget to
evaluate because it requires no action.

**Facts.** The offer is 18% more. Commute goes from 20 minutes to 55. The team
is six people; I have not met four of them.

**Assumptions.** The new role has the scope described — every role does, in the
interview. My current role will stay as it is if I stay, which is the least
examined assumption here and almost never true. The commute will bother me about
as much as I imagine.

**Opinion.** That I have "stopped learning." Real, and worth taking seriously,
but it is a feeling and it has been true before in roles that later got better.

**Unknown that would change the answer.** Whether the scope is real. Everything
else is preference; this is fact-shaped and checkable.

## bet-evaluate

**Confidence.** Moderate on the money, low on everything that will actually
determine whether this is a good two years.

**Outside view.** People systematically overweight salary and underweight
commute and colleagues in job choices, and report regretting exactly that.

**Stakes.** Two years of working life. Financial upside is modest and certain;
the downside — a worse day, every day — is larger and harder to price. Cost of
delay: the offer expires in a week, which is real pressure and also exactly the
condition under which people decide badly.

**Reversibility.** Harder than it looks. Leaving a new role inside a year is
costly to explain and to live through.

**Alternatives.** Take it. Decline. Renegotiate scope where I am, using the
offer. Higher-upside challenger: ask the new company for two conversations with
the engineers I would work beside — cheap, and it attacks the unknown that
actually decides this.

**Size.** Low confidence, high personal downside, hard to reverse → gather
evidence, and the deadline is not a reason to skip it.

## bet-experiment

**Deciding uncertainty:** is the scope real, and do I want to work with these
people?

**Probe:** two 30-minute conversations with prospective teammates, asking what
the last three months actually looked like rather than what the role is. One
conversation with my current manager about scope, which also tests the
assumption that staying means staying unchanged.

**Criteria.** Teammates describe work matching the pitch, unprompted → the
scope is real. Vague or contradictory answers → it is a story.

## bet-update

**Committed:** accepted, after two conversations that were specific and
consistent, and a current-role conversation that offered nothing concrete.

**Update criteria — set deliberately, because this is where resulting bites.**
At three months: is the work what they described? At six: is the commute
costing more than I priced? Written down before starting, precisely so the
answer is not reconstructed later from how it happens to feel.

## bet-review — at six months

The work matched. The commute is worse than expected and is costing about four
hours a week I had not priced.

**Good decision, mixed outcome.** The reasoning was sound and the checkable
unknown was checked. The commute was a known, deliberately accepted cost that
turned out at the bad end of the range — not an oversight.

**Belief updated:** I underprice recurring daily costs relative to one-off ones.
Applies to the next housing decision as much as the next job.
