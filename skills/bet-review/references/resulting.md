# Resulting

Judging a decision by how it turned out. Duke's term, and the reason experience
so often fails to sharpen judgement.

The mechanism is simple. Outcomes are loud and immediate; reasoning quality is
quiet and has to be reconstructed. So the outcome becomes the grade. Over time
this teaches people to avoid well-reasoned bets that happened to lose, and to
repeat sloppy calls that happened to win.

## The four quadrants

**Good decision, good outcome.** Check the reasoning actually caused it. A team
picks a boring, well-understood database for a workload that turns out to be
boring and well-understood. Sound, and confirmed. But the same call would look
identical if the workload had been unusual and the database had coped by luck —
so ask whether the reasoning was load-bearing before banking the lesson.

**Good decision, bad outcome.** A company runs a properly designed pilot, gets
a clear positive signal, rolls out, and the market moves underneath them six
weeks later. The decision was right on what was available. Nothing about the
process should change. Teams that miss this become risk-averse in exactly the
situations where a well-reasoned bet was correct — and the cost never appears
in a postmortem, because you cannot see the good bets that stopped being made.

**Bad decision, good outcome.** The dangerous one, because nothing triggers a
review. A founder skips diligence on a key hire because they liked them, and
the hire is excellent. The lesson learned is "trust your gut." The lesson
available was "we got away with one." Unexamined lucky wins become next year's
confident precedent, and the precedent is what fails.

**Bad decision, bad outcome.** The straightforward case. Still requires finding
the *specific* failure — an unchecked assumption, an alternative never
considered, evidence that was reachable and nobody reached for it — rather than
concluding that it went badly and resolving to be more careful.

## The distinguishing question

*Would a thoughtful person, with only what was available at the time, have
decided differently?*

If no, the decision was sound regardless of the result. If yes, name what they
would have done and whether it was actually reachable then — at a cost worth
paying, in the time available. "They should have known" is only fair if
knowing was possible.

## Outcomes are evidence, just weak evidence

This is not an argument for ignoring results. One outcome is a data point about
the world and should move your beliefs — a little. A single result rarely
justifies a large update, and a run of results justifies more than the same
count considered one at a time.

The failure is treating one outcome as decisive. That is resulting dressed up
as learning.

## Reviewing wins

Most teams review failures only, which guarantees the lucky calls are never
caught and the sound-but-unlucky ones absorb all the scrutiny. If reviews only
ever happen after something goes wrong, the sample is biased in the exact
direction that produces resulting.
