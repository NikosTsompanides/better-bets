# Probe catalogue

Ordered roughly by cost. Reach for the cheapest one that would actually move
you.

**Read what already happened.** Someone has usually tried this. A postmortem, a
case study, a conference talk, an issue thread. Hours, not weeks, and it costs
nothing but attention.

**Back-of-envelope model.** Write the arithmetic. Many decisions dissolve when
the numbers are on paper — the volume that "needs" a queue turns out to be 40
events a minute.

**Fake door.** Put the entry point in front of real people and count who tries
it. Answers demand questions that surveys answer badly, because stated
preference and revealed preference diverge.

**Five conversations.** Not a survey. Five specific people, asked what they
currently *do* about the problem, not what they would like. The most useful
question is "what have you already tried, and what did it cost you?"

**Timeboxed spike.** One day, one question, throwaway code. The output is an
answer, not an artifact. Agree in advance that it gets deleted.

**Benchmark on real data.** Synthetic benchmarks measure your synthetic data.
Use a real slice or accept that the result transfers poorly.

**Concierge version.** Do it by hand for a handful of users before building
anything. Slow and unscalable is the point — you learn whether it is wanted
before you learn how to automate it.

**Paid trial task.** For hiring, a short paid piece of real work tells you more
than another interview round, and respects the candidate's time enough that
good ones agree.

**Pilot with one team.** Contained blast radius, real conditions. Pick a team
that will tell you the truth rather than the friendliest one.

**Small-scale rollout.** A percentage of traffic or one segment, with a defined
stop rule.

**A/B test.** Only when you have the volume to reach significance in a
reasonable window. Below that it is a slow coin flip that looks like evidence.

## Choosing

Two questions: what is the smallest thing that would change my mind, and how
fast can I have it? Evidence-per-day beats thoroughness, because a slower
better experiment often arrives after the decision had to be made anyway.
