# Bias catalogue

Not a checklist to recite. These are the specific ways decisions go wrong, and
what each looks like from the inside.

**Confirmation bias.** You look for support, not tests. *Tell:* every piece of
evidence gathered so far points the same way. Real inquiry is messier.
*Counter:* ask what would prove you wrong, then go look there specifically.

**Anchoring.** The first number or framing sets the range. *Tell:* the options
are all variations on the first idea. *Counter:* generate an option deliberately
far from the anchor before comparing.

**Sunk cost.** Past investment justifies continuing. *Tell:* the argument
includes how much has already been spent. *Counter:* ask whether you would start
this today, knowing what you know, with the spend already gone.

**Outcome bias — "resulting".** Judging the decision by how it turned out. This
is the one Duke names, and it is the reason decision-making does not improve
with experience: good outcomes validate sloppy process and bad outcomes
discredit sound process. *Counter:* judge on the information available at the
time. `bet-review` exists for this.

**Availability.** Recent or vivid cases dominate. *Tell:* the reasoning leans on
one memorable incident. *Counter:* ask for the base rate.

**Premature convergence.** The first plausible option becomes the plan. *Tell:*
the alternatives were written after the choice, to justify it. *Counter:*
require a second real option before evaluating any.

**Overconfidence.** Estimates are too narrow. *Tell:* the range has no room for
the ordinary things that go wrong. *Counter:* the wager test, and the outside
view.

**Opinion laundered as fact.** A judgment repeats until it is treated as
established. *Tell:* nobody can say where it came from. *Counter:* ask what
would need to be true, and whether anyone checked.

## Biases specific to working with an AI

**Fluency as evidence.** A well-structured, confident answer reads as
well-founded. It is not. Presentation quality and grounding are independent.

**Agreement as validation.** An agent that agrees with you has not confirmed
anything — it has continued your framing, which is what the training rewards.
Two agreeing agents are not two witnesses.

**Plausible detail.** Generated specifics — a version number, a config flag, a
market statistic — feel like retrieved facts. Unless the agent actually checked,
they are assumptions with a confident voice.

**Solution momentum.** Asked how to build X, an agent will build X well rather
than ask whether X is the thing to build. The question "is this the right
problem?" has to be asked deliberately; it does not arise on its own.

## Proportion

Do not run these on a decision that does not warrant it. Firing the full
catalogue at a reversible small call is how a useful practice becomes something
people route around.
